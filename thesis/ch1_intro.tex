\label{ch:intro}

\section{Background and Motivation}
Financial markets represent notoriously difficult environments for autonomous sequential decision-making. Daily asset price movements are characterised by low signal-to-noise ratios, non-stationary underlying return distributions, and partial observability of the true macroeconomic and order-flow state. An algorithmic trading agent observing historical prices and volumes never directly observes the complete market environment, but only a noisy, lossy projection of it.

Portfolio allocation is an intrinsically sequential problem: the economic outcome of an allocation decision depends critically on the history of prior allocations and on how portfolio positions are actively rebalanced over time, rather than on isolated, one-step directional forecasts. This sequential nature makes Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) a natural paradigm for portfolio management, as DRL agents learn to optimize cumulative, long-horizon risk-adjusted rewards under market uncertainty rather than one-step predictive accuracy.

However, practical algorithmic execution requires rigorous accounting for transaction frictions. DRL agents operating in continuous action spaces frequently exhibit excessive reallocation or churning: policies that appear superficially profitable before transaction fees can incur severe losses once realistic transaction costs are subtracted from portfolio wealth. While extensive prior literature has separately proposed temporal neural architectures \citep{zou2023novel}, risk-aware reward objectives \citep{millea2021deep, wang2025risk}, and dynamic walk-forward evaluation protocols \citep{liu2024dynamic}, these mechanisms are almost never isolated within a single, consistent experimental environment. Differences in datasets, transaction cost models, feature sets, and evaluation frameworks across published studies make it nearly impossible to discern whether reported empirical gains arise from the neural architecture, reward formulation, turnover suppression, or benign testing conditions.

\section{Research Objectives}
To address this limitation, the specific objectives of this B.Tech.\ project are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Controlled Four-Stage Ablation:} To design and implement a single-factor controlled four-stage ablation framework---Model M1: PPO (MLP), Model M2: LSTM-PPO, Model M3: LSTM-PPO with Differential Sharpe Ratio (DSR) reward, and Model M4: LSTM-PPO with DSR and turnover regularisation---that isolates the incremental impact of temporal memory, risk-aware reward shaping, and turnover control on a continuous, Dirichlet-parameterised portfolio allocation policy under identical data, cost, and training conditions.
    \item \textbf{Unified POMDP Environment and Protocol:} To define a single, rigorous Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) trading environment incorporating causal feature engineering, exact proportional transaction cost accounting, and a strict chronological walk-forward evaluation protocol across eight years of Indian equity data (2018--2025).
    \item \textbf{Multi-Dimensional Empirical Evaluation:} To evaluate all models across a comprehensive set of cost-aware, risk-adjusted metrics---including cumulative profit, annualized return, Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, maximum drawdown, turnover, and cumulative transaction costs---to determine which mechanisms genuinely contribute to robust out-of-sample performance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
This thesis investigates and answers four primary research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Temporal Architecture):} Does recurrent temporal modeling via an LSTM encoder improve out-of-sample risk-adjusted returns and policy stability compared to a static MLP baseline?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Risk-Aware Reward Shaping):} Does replacing raw net portfolio returns with the recursive online Differential Sharpe Ratio (DSR) improve Sharpe ratios and reduce maximum drawdowns?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Turnover Regularisation):} Does augmenting the reward with an explicit quadratic turnover penalty effectively suppress excessive trading churn and transaction costs without destroying wealth accumulation?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Benchmark Competitiveness):} Can a continuous actor-critic DRL agent outperform a passive Equal-Weight benchmark in a realistic, friction-constrained Indian equity market?
\end{itemize}
